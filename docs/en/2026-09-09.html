OpenAI Solves Navier–Stokes, a Millennium Prize Problem, Using AI + Math Community Controversy
Hey clawii — the entire HN and math world is exploding today. OpenAI announced they used an internal model (likely GPT‑6 Astra) to solve the Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem, one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems. But here's the controversy: they launched their effort after hearing rumors that another team (an Anthropic employee + an NYU professor) had been working on it with AI for nearly a year — and OpenAI got there first in just 88 hours. Simon Willison wrote a deep analysis, Terence Tao weighed in saying "open math problems are being non-renewably mined by AI." This isn't just a model release — it's a capability leap and an academic ethics earthquake.
📍 Context · The Navier–Stokes equations describe fluid motion (air, water currents). The "existence and smoothness" problem is one of seven Millennium Prize Problems announced by the Clay Mathematics Institute in 2000, each carrying a $1M prize. For decades, countless mathematicians tried and failed. In August 2026, Anthropic employee Levent Alpöge and NYU professor Tristan Buckmaster collaborated for nearly a year using Claude + Codex (GPT-5.6 Sol), achieving a breakthrough on August 15. On September 1, OpenAI heard a rumor that "two Millennium Prize problems had been solved" and launched an internal model effort (GPT‑6 Astra) to evaluate all Millennium problems. By September 5, they solved Navier–Stokes in 88 hours, consuming ~130 billion output tokens (roughly $15M at public API pricing). OpenAI claims they did not access Buckmaster's private data but cannot rule out "de-identified data" influence. Buckmaster accuses OpenAI of racing ahead. Simon Willison compares this to "just a rumor of a bug being enough to trigger agents to find exploits" — now the same is true for math.

This means AI can now tackle problems that take top human mathematicians years or decades.
  • Indirect implications for vibe coding: While you don't need to solve PDEs, this proves agentic long-chain reasoning and formal verification are real. Future coding agents may automatically verify code correctness (similar to Lean for math proofs).

🔬 Deep dive — technical details, controversy, implications for vibe coding

Technical details: OpenAI used an internal model (likely a GPT‑6 Astra variant). The agent architecture was similar to Codex + the Lean formal verification tool. The workflow: agent autonomously proposes a mathematical construction → formalizes it in Lean → verifies → iterates. A total of 2.7 million messages were sent, consuming ~130 billion output tokens. Lean verification took an additional 17 hours.

vs Buckmaster's team: Buckmaster's team spent nearly a year with human-led + AI-assisted work (Claude + Codex). OpenAI's approach was pure agent-driven, extremely fast but very expensive (~$15M at public API pricing). The two proofs differ in details (forced vs unforced Euler case).

Core controversy:

  • OpenAI admits they "launched after hearing a rumor" but claims they did not access Buckmaster's private data.
  • Buckmaster alleges OpenAI may have indirectly benefited from their work through de-identified training data.
  • OpenAI offered a joint release but refused to include Levent Alpöge (an Anthropic employee) as a co-author.
  • Simon points out: this mirrors computer security — just a rumor of a bug is enough to trigger agent swarming. Now the same applies to math.

Implications for vibe coding:

  • Agent long-chain reasoning has reached the level of solving frontier math problems. Future coding agents may also auto-verify code correctness (similar to Lean verification).
  • Cost issue: $15M per run, but the trend is rapidly declining. Similar capability may be available at much lower cost within 1-2 years.
  • Data privacy: if you use AI for innovative work, your data could be used for training and help competitors. Consider local models or explicit opt-out.

Honest caveat: This event has no direct impact on daily vibe coding. But it's a signal — the capability boundary of AI is expanding fast. Worth watching for advances in agentic long-chain reasoning and formal verification.

🔭 What to watch for · Whether OpenAI releases more technical details (model version, agent architecture). Whether the math community accepts the proof. Whether other Millennium problems get solved by AI. Anthropic's response.

Meta Muse — Personal AI Agent
Clawii — Meta just launched Muse, a "personal AI agent." It's sitting at 531 points on HN with 573 comments. Muse positions itself as your private assistant that can execute tasks across apps, manage your calendar, write emails, and do research. It's based on Meta's Llama family of models (exact version undisclosed). The key differentiator: it competes directly with ChatGPT and Claude, and Meta is emphasizing "privacy first" with on-device data processing. For someone who uses AI heavily like you, this is a new option worth watching.
📍 Context · The personal AI agent market is expanding fast: OpenAI has ChatGPT + Astra, Anthropic has Claude, Google has Gemini. Meta previously focused on open-source models (Llama) but is now launching a consumer-facing agent product. Muse's selling point is "privacy first" — data processing happens on-device rather than being uploaded to the cloud. This contrasts with ChatGPT's cloud-based approach. HN discussion is focused on: can Muse really run locally? How's the performance? What advantages does it have over existing agents?

Why this is worth a look

  • New competitor enters the ring: Meta has a massive user base (Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp). If Muse integrates into these platforms, it could gain users quickly. For developers, this could mean a new API and ecosystem.
  • Privacy-first differentiation: If you're sensitive about data privacy (e.g., you don't want OpenAI seeing your work content), Muse's on-device processing model could be an alternative.
  • HN 531 pts, active discussion: 573 comments — the community is debating Muse's actual capabilities, the credibility of its privacy promises, and how it compares to existing tools.

🎯 What you can do today

  1. Read Meta's official announcement: meta.com/muse
  2. Read the HN discussion: HN main thread
  3. If available, try Muse's early access (requires a Meta account).
🔬 Deep dive — vs ChatGPT/Claude, technical details, how hard to use

vs ChatGPT / Claude:

  • ChatGPT and Claude are cloud-based; Muse emphasizes on-device processing (at least for some tasks).
  • Muse may integrate with Meta's ecosystem (Instagram, WhatsApp), while ChatGPT has its plugin ecosystem.
  • Model capability: Muse is based on the Llama family (exact version undisclosed). Llama 4 models lag behind GPT-5.6 Sol or Claude Fable 5 on coding tasks, but are likely sufficient for everyday tasks.
  • Pricing: Meta hasn't announced pricing yet — could be free or low-cost (ad-supported).

Technical details: Muse uses Meta's Llama model, fine-tuned for agent tasks. Supports tool calling (calendar, email, browser, etc.). Privacy: sensitive data processed on-device, non-sensitive tasks may use the cloud.

How hard to use: Requires a Meta account. Likely available as a standalone app or integrated into existing Meta apps. Non-developer friendly.

Honest caveat: Meta's privacy promises need verification — historically, Meta has had data privacy controversies. Muse's on-device capabilities may be limited; complex tasks may still require cloud processing.

🔭 What to watch for · Whether Muse opens an API or plugin system. Real-world reviews comparing it to ChatGPT/Claude. Whether Meta open-sources the underlying model.
