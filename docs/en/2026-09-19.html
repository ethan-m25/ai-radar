"How to Write with an LLM" — Thomas Ptacek's Guide Becomes Today's Most-Read AI Post

Hey clawii — you use LLMs every day to draft, edit, and polish text for compensation reports, emails, and analyses. Thomas Ptacek just published a guide with one rule that might change how you work: "You may not use a single word an LLM suggests to you." It hit HN at 497 points and Simon Willison endorsed it.

📍 Context: Thomas Ptacek is a well-known security researcher and longtime Hacker News fixture. His guide distinguishes between using LLMs as writing assistants (letting them generate text for you) vs. copyeditors (using them to improve text you already wrote). His core argument: the single biggest mistake people make is letting LLMs write prose, because the output has a generic, soulless "AI smell." Instead, use LLMs for fact-checking, spelling, grammar, and vocabulary — but never adopt their phrasing. Simon Willison, who runs one of the most widely-read AI blogs, called the rule "good" and shared his own similar workflow. The post has been on the HN front page for over 24 hours with 337 comments, making it the most discussed AI article today by a wide margin.

Why this matters

  • This is the most practical AI advice you'll read this month — not about a new model or tool, but about how to use what you already have (ChatGPT, Claude, Codex) differently. Ptacek's key insight: LLMs are brilliant at editing and terrible at writing. Use them for what they're good at.
  • Directly applicable to your daily work — you write compensation analyses, policy memos, email negotiations. Ptacek provides a ready-to-use workflow: write your draft → paste into ChatGPT/Claude → ask for factual accuracy checks → spelling/grammar fixes → thesaurus suggestions. Accept none of the AI's phrasing. Your voice stays yours.
  • Simon Willison's endorsement adds weight — he wrote: "I use them for fact-checking, spelling and grammar and as an occasional thesaurus... The rule to never use a turn of phrase suggested by an LLM feels good to me." Willison is one of the most practical AI commentators; if he says it works, it works.
  • High signal density on HN — 497 points, 337 comments, front page for 24+ hours. The discussion thread is unusually substantive, with many commenters sharing their own workflows and variations on Ptacek's approach.

🎯 What you can do today

  1. Read the full guide at sockpuppet.org (no paywall). It's a 10-minute read. The system prompt Ptacek shares at the bottom is worth bookmarking.
  2. Try his workflow on Monday — draft your next report or email yourself, then feed it to ChatGPT or Claude with this prompt: "Check this for factual accuracy, spelling, grammar, and suggest alternative word choices. Do NOT rewrite any sentences for me." Accept the edits you agree with, ignore the rest.
  3. Share the link with your team — the biggest risk with AI in a corporate setting is everyone producing same-sounding, AI-smelling documents. Ptacek's approach preserves individual voice while still leveraging AI's strengths.
🔬 Deep dive — how Ptacek's approach differs from normal ChatGPT usage
DimensionNormal ChatGPT usagePtacek's approach
Who writesYou give instructions, AI writes textYou write everything, AI edits your text
VoiceGeneric AI tone (even with style instructions)Your natural voice, preserved

What it runs on: Any LLM — ChatGPT, Claude, Codex. No special setup needed. Just a system prompt and discipline.

How hard to use: Zero setup. Open ChatGPT, paste your text, paste the system prompt, done.

Honest caveat: This approach is slower than letting the AI write for you. You still do the creative work. The speed gain comes from not having to rewrite AI-generated text — which most people spend a lot of time doing.

🔭 Watch for: If this approach gets adopted in corporate settings, it could change how companies train employees on AI use. The next signal would be a major company publishing internal guidelines that echo Ptacek's rule ("never use AI-generated phrasing").
